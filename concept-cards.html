1 · Text Preprocessing
PRE-01 Sentence Segmentation
DefinitionThe task of splitting a block of raw text into individual sentences.
PurposeMost downstream NLP steps (parsing, summarization, translation) operate one sentence at a time, so the sentence boundary has to be found first.
Working PrincipleRule-based splitters look for boundary punctuation (. ! ?) followed by capitalisation, while handling exceptions like abbreviations ("Dr.", "e.g.") and decimal points; statistical/ML splitters instead learn boundary cues from labelled corpora.
Example
Input: "Dr. Rao joined NASA in 2019. She leads the NLP team." Output: ["Dr. Rao joined NASA in 2019.", "She leads the NLP team."]
AdvantagesGives every later step a clean, self-contained unit of meaning; simple rule-based versions are fast and need no training data.
LimitationsAbbreviations, ellipses, URLs, and informal punctuation (emojis, "...") routinely break simple rule-based splitters.
ApplicationsMachine translation, text-to-speech, summarization, and any pipeline that parses grammar sentence-by-sentence.
PRE-02 Tokenization
DefinitionBreaking text into smaller units — words, sub-words, or characters — called tokens.
PurposeModels cannot operate on raw character streams; they need discrete units to count, embed, and process.
Working PrincipleWhitespace/rule-based tokenizers split on spaces and punctuation; sub-word tokenizers (BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece) learn a vocabulary of frequent character chunks so rare words are broken into known pieces instead of being discarded.
Example
Input: "NLP is not that hard!" Output: ["NLP", "is", "not", "that", "hard", "!"]
AdvantagesCreates the atomic unit every later technique (BoW, embeddings, transformers) depends on; sub-word schemes handle unseen words gracefully.
LimitationsWord boundaries are ambiguous in languages without spaces (Chinese, Japanese, Thai); contractions and hyphenation need special handling.
ApplicationsThe first step of virtually every NLP pipeline — search engines, chatbots, translators, and large language models.
PRE-03 Case Normalization
DefinitionConverting all text to a single letter case, almost always lowercase.
PurposeWithout it, "Apple", "apple", and "APPLE" are treated as three distinct tokens, inflating the vocabulary and splitting statistics that should be shared.
Working PrincipleA direct character-mapping operation (e.g. Python's .lower()) applied uniformly across the corpus, usually right after tokenization.
Example
Input: "Apple released the iPhone. APPLE stock rose." Output: "apple released the iphone. apple stock rose."
AdvantagesShrinks vocabulary size and merges duplicate counts, which is especially valuable for frequency-based methods like Bag of Words and TF-IDF.
LimitationsDestroys information: "Apple" (company) vs "apple" (fruit), or "US" vs "us", becomes indistinguishable — a real cost for Named Entity Recognition.
ApplicationsSearch indexing, spam filtering, and any frequency-based feature pipeline where case carries little meaning.
PRE-04 Stop-word Removal
DefinitionDiscarding very common, low-information words (e.g. "the", "is", "and", "of") from the token stream.
PurposeThese words appear in almost every document and carry little topical signal, so keeping them mostly adds noise and computation.
Working PrincipleEach token is checked against a fixed or corpus-derived stop-word list and dropped if it matches.
Example
Input: ["the", "cat", "is", "sitting", "on", "the", "mat"] Output: ["cat", "sitting", "mat"]
AdvantagesReduces feature-space size and speeds up downstream computation, particularly for Bag of Words and TF-IDF.
LimitationsCan remove words that matter for meaning or grammar ("not", "no") — a serious problem for sentiment analysis and negation-sensitive tasks; also language- and domain-dependent.
ApplicationsClassical information retrieval, topic modelling, and keyword-based search, though modern embedding-based methods increasingly keep stop-words for context.
PRE-05 Stemming
DefinitionChopping word endings using heuristic rules to reduce a word to a rough base or "stem".
PurposeMerges inflected forms of a word ("running", "runs", "ran"-ish) so they count as the same feature.
Working PrincipleRule-based suffix-stripping algorithms (e.g. the Porter Stemmer) apply a cascade of pattern-matching rules like "remove -ing, -ed, -es" without consulting a dictionary or the word's grammatical role.
Example
"studies" → "studi" "studying" → "studi" "connection", "connected", "connecting" → "connect"
AdvantagesExtremely fast, needs no dictionary or context, and works well enough for search/indexing at scale.
LimitationsThe result is often not a real word ("studi"), and unrelated words can collide (over-stemming) while related ones can be missed (under-stemming).
ApplicationsSearch engines and information retrieval systems where speed matters more than linguistic precision.
PRE-06 Lemmatization
DefinitionReducing a word to its dictionary base form (its "lemma") using vocabulary and grammar rather than raw pattern-cutting.
PurposeAchieves the same normalization goal as stemming but keeps the result a valid, meaningful word.
Working PrincipleUses a lexicon and, typically, part-of-speech information to map a word to its lemma — "better" (adjective) → "good", "running" (verb) → "run" — often via morphological analysis or a lookup dictionary such as WordNet.
Example
"studies" → "study" "was", "were", "is" → "be" "better" → "good"
AdvantagesMore linguistically accurate than stemming, produces real words, and preserves meaning distinctions stemming would blur.
LimitationsSlower, requires POS tagging and a lexical resource, and needs language-specific dictionaries to work well.
ApplicationsChatbots, question answering, and any task where the readability or grammatical correctness of the normalized form matters.
PRE-07 Noise Removal
DefinitionStripping out elements of raw text that are not part of the meaningful language signal — HTML tags, special characters, extra whitespace, boilerplate.
PurposeReal-world text (scraped web pages, PDFs, social posts) is full of markup and artefacts that confuse tokenizers and inflate vocabularies.
Working PrinciplePattern matching and regular expressions (or dedicated parsers, e.g. an HTML parser) identify and delete non-linguistic spans while preserving the surrounding text.
Example
Input: "<p>Great product!!! 😀 Visit http://shop.example.com</p>" Output: "Great product Visit"
AdvantagesPrevents markup and artefacts from being mistaken for vocabulary, improving both accuracy and efficiency downstream.
LimitationsAggressive noise removal can delete signal along with noise — emojis and punctuation can carry sentiment, and URLs may be informative for spam detection.
ApplicationsWeb-scraped corpora, social-media analytics, and any pipeline ingesting HTML, PDFs, or OCR output.
PRE-08 Text Cleaning
DefinitionThe umbrella process that combines noise removal, normalization, and correction steps into a single standardisation pass over raw text.
PurposeEnsures every document entering the pipeline follows the same conventions, so later statistical steps compare like with like.
Working PrincipleChains together sub-steps — lowercasing, punctuation handling, whitespace collapsing, spelling correction, encoding fixes — in a defined, repeatable order.
Example
Input: "I LOVE this!! Itss amazingg :)" Output: "i love this it is amazing"
AdvantagesProduces a consistent, model-ready corpus and reduces the "garbage in, garbage out" risk that plagues raw-text pipelines.
LimitationsOver-cleaning can erase stylistic or emotional signal (e.g. repeated letters expressing enthusiasm) that some tasks — like sentiment analysis — actually want to keep.
ApplicationsAny production NLP system as the mandatory first stage before feature engineering or model input.
2 · Feature Engineering
FEAT-01 Bag of Words (BoW)
DefinitionA representation of text as an unordered collection of word counts, ignoring grammar and word order.
PurposeConverts variable-length text into a fixed-length numeric vector that classic machine-learning models can consume.
Working PrincipleBuild a vocabulary of all unique words in the corpus, then represent each document as a vector counting how many times each vocabulary word appears in it.
Example
Vocabulary: [cat, sat, mat, dog] "the cat sat on the mat" → [1, 1, 1, 0] "the dog sat" → [0, 1, 0, 1]
AdvantagesSimple, fast to compute, and works reasonably well for topic-level classification tasks like spam detection.
LimitationsDiscards word order and context ("dog bites man" = "man bites dog"), and produces very high-dimensional, sparse vectors for large vocabularies.
ApplicationsSpam filtering, basic document classification, and as a baseline before trying richer representations.
FEAT-02 N-Grams
DefinitionA contiguous sequence of N tokens taken from text (N=1 unigram, N=2 bigram, N=3 trigram, etc.).
PurposeRecovers some of the local word-order information that Bag of Words throws away, by treating short word sequences as features in their own right.
Working PrincipleA sliding window of size N moves across the token stream, and every window position becomes one N-gram feature/count.
Example
"I love NLP" Unigrams: [I, love, NLP] Bigrams: [I love, love NLP]
AdvantagesCaptures short phrases and local word order ("not good" vs "good"), improving results for tasks sensitive to word sequence.
LimitationsVocabulary size grows combinatorially as N increases, quickly leading to sparse, memory-hungry feature spaces.
ApplicationsStatistical language models, autocomplete/predictive text, and plagiarism or authorship-style detection.
FEAT-03 Term Frequency (TF)
DefinitionA measure of how often a term appears within one specific document, usually normalised by that document's length.
PurposeCaptures how central a word is to a single document's content — the more a word repeats, the more it likely matters there.
Working PrincipleTF(t, d) = (number of times term t appears in document d) ÷ (total number of terms in d).
Example
Doc: "the cat sat on the mat" (6 words) TF("the") = 2/6 = 0.33 TF("cat") = 1/6 = 0.17
AdvantagesSimple, interpretable, and length-normalised so documents of different sizes remain comparable.
LimitationsTreats every word equally important as long as it repeats — common words like "the" score highly despite carrying no topical meaning.
ApplicationsThe building block of TF-IDF; used wherever within-document word importance needs to be quantified.
FEAT-04 Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)
DefinitionA measure of how rare or distinctive a term is across an entire document collection (corpus).
PurposeDown-weights words that appear in nearly every document (little discriminative value) and up-weights words that appear in only a few (highly discriminative).
Working PrincipleIDF(t) = log( total number of documents ÷ number of documents containing term t ). Common words get a low score; rare words get a high score.
Example
Corpus of 1,000 docs. "the" appears in 950 docs → IDF ≈ log(1000/950) ≈ 0.02 "quasar" appears in 3 docs → IDF ≈ log(1000/3) ≈ 2.52
AdvantagesAutomatically identifies which words are actually distinctive for a corpus without any hand-built stop-word list.
LimitationsNeeds the whole corpus statistics up front, so it does not adapt well to a single new document or a streaming setting without recomputation.
ApplicationsPaired with TF to form TF-IDF; used in search-engine relevance ranking and keyword extraction.
FEAT-05 TF-IDF
DefinitionA composite score, TF × IDF, that weighs a term by how often it appears in a document and how rare it is across the corpus.
PurposeSurfaces the words that are both frequent within a document and distinctive across the collection — the words that actually characterise that document.
Working PrincipleTF-IDF(t, d) = TF(t, d) × IDF(t). A word scores highest when it repeats often in one document but rarely elsewhere.
Example
"the": high TF, near-zero IDF → low TF-IDF (unimportant) "quasar": modest TF, high IDF → high TF-IDF (distinctive)
AdvantagesAutomatically balances frequency against distinctiveness, is cheap to compute, and remains a strong baseline for search and classification.
LimitationsStill produces sparse, high-dimensional vectors and, like Bag of Words, ignores word order and semantic similarity between related words.
ApplicationsSearch-engine ranking, document similarity, keyword extraction, and classic text-classification baselines.
3 · Language Representation
REP-01 One-Hot Encoding
DefinitionRepresenting each word as a vector the size of the vocabulary, with a single 1 at that word's index and 0 everywhere else.
PurposeThe most basic way to turn categorical, symbolic words into numeric vectors a model can process.
Working PrincipleAssign every vocabulary word a unique index; its vector is all zeros except a 1 at that index.
Example
Vocabulary: [cat, dog, fish] (size 3) "cat" → [1, 0, 0] "dog" → [0, 1, 0]
AdvantagesTrivial to construct and completely unambiguous — every word maps to exactly one vector.
LimitationsVector size equals vocabulary size (extremely high-dimensional and sparse), and it treats every pair of words as equally (dis)similar — "cat" and "dog" are as unrelated as "cat" and "sky".
ApplicationsSmall, fixed-vocabulary categorical features; largely superseded by embeddings for open-vocabulary language tasks.
REP-02 Word Embeddings
DefinitionDense, low-dimensional vector representations of words learned so that words with similar meaning end up close together in the vector space.
PurposeOvercomes one-hot encoding's core weakness by capturing semantic similarity numerically, not just identity.
Working PrincipleA model is trained on large text corpora to predict a word from its context (or vice versa); the internal weights it learns become each word's embedding vector, typically 50–300 dimensions.
Example
vector("king") - vector("man") + vector("woman") ≈ vector("queen") distance(cat, dog) is small; distance(cat, spreadsheet) is large
AdvantagesCompact, captures semantic and even analogical relationships, and generalises to unseen combinations of known words.
LimitationsTraditional embeddings assign one fixed vector per word regardless of context, so a polysemous word like "bank" gets a single blended meaning.
ApplicationsSearch relevance, recommendation, clustering, and as the input layer for most modern neural NLP models.
REP-03 Word2Vec
DefinitionA neural technique for learning word embeddings using shallow (single hidden-layer) prediction tasks over local context windows.
PurposeMade high-quality, semantically meaningful word embeddings practical to train on very large corpora efficiently.
Working PrincipleTwo architectures: CBOW predicts a target word from its surrounding context words, while Skip-gram predicts the surrounding context words from a target word; the hidden-layer weights become the embeddings.
Example
Skip-gram on "the quick brown fox jumps" target "brown" → predict {quick, fox} as nearby context
AdvantagesFast to train even on billions of words, and produces embeddings that capture strong semantic and syntactic regularities.
LimitationsEach word still gets exactly one vector (no context-sensitivity), and it cannot represent words absent from training (out-of-vocabulary problem).
ApplicationsSemantic search, recommendation systems, and as pretrained input embeddings for downstream neural models.
REP-04 FastText
DefinitionAn extension of Word2Vec that represents each word as a bag of character N-grams (sub-word units) rather than a single atomic token.
PurposeSolves Word2Vec's out-of-vocabulary problem and better handles morphologically rich languages (many prefixes/suffixes).
Working PrincipleA word's embedding is the sum of the embeddings of its constituent character N-grams, so unseen words can still get a reasonable vector built from familiar sub-word pieces.
Example
"apple" (n=3) → <ap, app, ppl, ple, le> embedding("apple") = sum of embeddings of these character n-grams
AdvantagesHandles rare and unseen words gracefully and captures morphological relationships (e.g. "run", "running", "runner" share sub-word pieces).
LimitationsLarger model size due to the sub-word vocabulary, and, like Word2Vec, still produces one fixed vector per word without contextual disambiguation.
ApplicationsMorphologically rich languages (e.g. Finnish, Tamil, Turkish), spelling-variant-tolerant search, and low-resource language NLP.
REP-05 Contextual Embeddings
DefinitionWord representations that change depending on the surrounding sentence, so the same word gets a different vector in different contexts.
PurposeDirectly addresses the one-vector-per-word limitation of Word2Vec/FastText by letting meaning vary with context, as it does in real language.
Working PrincipleDeep models (e.g. ELMo's bidirectional LSTMs, or transformer encoders like BERT) process an entire sentence at once, producing a vector for each word that is a function of the whole surrounding sentence, not just the word itself.
Example
"He sat by the river bank." → embedding("bank") ≈ [financial=low, river=high] "He deposited cash at the bank." → embedding("bank") ≈ [financial=high, river=low]
AdvantagesResolves polysemy (multiple word senses) and captures nuanced, sentence-level meaning far beyond static embeddings.
LimitationsComputationally far more expensive to produce and store than static embeddings, since a word's vector must be recomputed every time its context changes.
ApplicationsQuestion answering, machine translation, sentiment analysis, and virtually every state-of-the-art NLP system today.
4 · Language Models
LM-01 Statistical Language Models
DefinitionModels that estimate the probability of a word (or sequence) using frequency counts observed directly in a training corpus.
PurposeProvide a way to score how "likely" a sentence is, useful for choosing between candidate outputs in speech recognition, translation, or autocomplete.
Working PrincipleN-gram models apply the Markov assumption — a word's probability depends only on the previous N-1 words — and estimate P(word | previous words) from corpus counts, often with smoothing for unseen sequences.
Example
Bigram model: P("world" | "hello") = count("hello world") / count("hello")
AdvantagesSimple, interpretable, computationally cheap, and effective for narrow, high-frequency-pattern domains.
LimitationsCannot capture long-range dependencies beyond the fixed window N, and probability tables explode in size and sparsity as N grows.
ApplicationsEarly speech recognition, spelling correction, and lightweight autocomplete on resource-constrained devices.
LM-02 Neural Language Models
DefinitionModels that use neural networks (feed-forward, then recurrent networks such as LSTMs/GRUs) to estimate word probabilities, learning distributed representations rather than relying on raw counts.
PurposeOvercome the sparsity and fixed-window limits of statistical N-gram models by generalising across similar words and remembering longer context.
Working PrincipleA neural network (e.g. an LSTM) reads a sequence of word embeddings one step at a time, maintaining an internal hidden state that carries information forward, and predicts the next word's probability distribution at each step.
Example
Input: "The cat sat on the ___" LSTM hidden state carries context across all prior words → predicts "mat" with high probability
AdvantagesCaptures longer-range dependencies than fixed N-grams and generalises to word combinations never literally seen in training.
LimitationsSequential processing is slow to train (each step depends on the previous one) and can still struggle with very long-range dependencies due to vanishing gradients.
ApplicationsEarly neural machine translation, text generation, and predictive keyboards before the transformer era.
LM-03 Transformer-based Representations
DefinitionLanguage representations produced by the transformer architecture, which uses a "self-attention" mechanism to relate every word in a sequence to every other word directly, without sequential recurrence.
PurposeRemove the sequential bottleneck of recurrent neural language models and capture long-range dependencies far more effectively, while allowing massive parallel training.
Working PrincipleSelf-attention computes, for every word, a weighted combination of all other words in the sequence based on learned relevance scores; stacking many such attention layers (as in BERT, GPT) builds deep, richly contextual representations of an entire input at once.
Example
"The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired." Attention links "it" strongly to "animal" (not "street"), resolving the reference correctly.
AdvantagesState-of-the-art accuracy across nearly all NLP tasks, highly parallelizable training, and representations that are fully contextual.
LimitationsVery large compute, memory, and data requirements; attention cost grows quadratically with sequence length, making very long documents expensive to process.
ApplicationsSearch engines, machine translation, chatbots and virtual assistants, summarization, and the foundation of today's large language models.
