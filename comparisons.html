Section B

Comparative Analysis

Some concepts are only fully understood next to their alternative. Five head-to-head tables, each scored on mechanism, computational cost, strengths, weaknesses, and best-fit use case.

1 · Stemming vs Lemmatization
DimensionStemmingLemmatization
Working mechanismRule-based suffix stripping (e.g. Porter's algorithm); no dictionary or grammar involved.Dictionary + morphological/POS analysis maps a word to its true lemma.
Computational complexityVery low — simple string pattern matching, O(length of word).Higher — needs POS tagging and lexicon lookup per word.
StrengthsExtremely fast; no external resources required; good enough for bulk indexing.Linguistically accurate; output is always a real, readable word.
WeaknessesFrequently produces non-words ("studi", "argu"); prone to over/under-stemming.Slower; requires a language-specific dictionary and correct POS tags to work well.
Suitable applicationsSearch engines, large-scale indexing, information retrieval.Chatbots, question answering, grammar-sensitive or user-facing text.
2 · TF vs TF-IDF
DimensionTF (Term Frequency)TF-IDF
Working mechanismCounts how often a term appears within a single document, normalised by document length.Multiplies TF by IDF, so a term's score also depends on how rare it is across the whole corpus.
Computational complexityLow — one pass over each document.Slightly higher — requires a corpus-wide document-frequency count in addition to per-document TF.
StrengthsSimple, cheap, and reflects a word's local importance.Automatically down-weights generic words and highlights genuinely distinctive terms.
WeaknessesCannot tell a genuinely important word from a merely common one (e.g. "the" scores high).Still ignores word order and semantics; needs the full corpus recomputed if new documents are added.
Suitable applicationsQuick within-document frequency analysis, word clouds.Search-engine ranking, keyword extraction, text-classification baselines.
3 · Word2Vec vs FastText
DimensionWord2VecFastText
Working mechanismLearns one dense vector per whole word via CBOW or Skip-gram context prediction.Represents a word as the sum of its character N-gram (sub-word) vectors, then trains those with a Word2Vec-style objective.
Computational complexityModerate; vocabulary-sized embedding matrix.Higher training and storage cost due to the larger sub-word N-gram vocabulary.
StrengthsFast to train, strong semantic and analogical structure for common words.Handles rare, misspelled, and unseen (out-of-vocabulary) words; strong for morphologically rich languages.
WeaknessesFails on out-of-vocabulary words; one fixed vector per word regardless of context.Larger model size; still produces one fixed vector per word (no context sensitivity).
Suitable applicationsSemantic search and recommendation on well-resourced languages with clean vocabularies.Low-resource and morphologically rich languages, spelling-variant-tolerant search.
4 · Statistical vs Neural Language Models
DimensionStatistical (N-gram) LMsNeural LMs
Working mechanismEstimate next-word probability from raw N-gram frequency counts under the Markov assumption.Use neural networks (RNN/LSTM, later transformers) to learn distributed representations and predict probabilities.
Computational complexityLow to train; probability tables grow sparse and huge as N increases.High — requires substantial compute and data, especially at transformer scale.
StrengthsSimple, interpretable, cheap, effective for narrow and high-frequency patterns.Generalises to unseen word combinations; captures long-range context and semantics.
WeaknessesCannot capture dependencies beyond the fixed window; severe data sparsity at higher N.Expensive to train and run; less interpretable ("black box") behaviour.
Suitable applicationsLightweight, resource-constrained settings (basic spelling correction, small autocomplete).Machine translation, chatbots, summarization, and modern large language models.
5 · One-Hot Encoding vs Word Embeddings
DimensionOne-Hot EncodingWord Embeddings
Working mechanismEach word gets a vector of vocabulary length with a single 1 marking its index.Each word gets a dense, low-dimensional vector learned so that similar words sit close together.
Computational complexityTrivial to construct, but vector size scales with vocabulary (very high-dimensional).Requires training (or loading a pretrained model), but produces compact, fixed-size vectors (e.g. 100–300 dims).
StrengthsSimple, unambiguous, no training needed.Captures semantic similarity and analogy; generalises across related words.
WeaknessesExtremely sparse and high-dimensional; treats all word pairs as equally unrelated.Needs a training corpus and compute; static embeddings still miss context-dependent meaning.
Suitable applicationsSmall, fixed categorical vocabularies (e.g. labels, tags).Open-vocabulary language tasks — search, recommendation, and as neural model input.
